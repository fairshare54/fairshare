Proof, not promises

Attack Lab: try to break it.

Any peer allocation system can be gamed. The honest question is how much gaming costs. This page runs the attack FairShare is built to resist: three people form a collusion ring and point only at each other. It runs with the exact settlement maths from the contract, and the numbers are the contract property test property_collusion_ring_earns_less_than_honest.

Why the ring earns less

Two forces work against the ring. First, breadth. The square root rewards points that arrive from many distinct givers. 100 points arriving from 2 givers scores 19600. The same 100 points arriving from 5 givers scores 48400. The trio in the ring round is fed by 2 givers each. The same trio in the honest round is fed by 6 givers each. Second, the reciprocity discount. Every mutual leg in the ring is cut by half of the shared amount, so the loop keeps less of what it passes around. A one way gift of 50 points scores 4900. The same 50 points given mutually scores 2500.

The result, from the property test: the ring trio earns 3.506 NEAR each on average, while the other six earn 8.246 NEAR each in the same round. In the honest round the same three people earn 15.000 NEAR each, capped, while the other six earn 2.499 NEAR each. Honest giving earns the trio 11.493 NEAR more per person than the ring did.

The honest limit of this test

This is a simulation, not a proof that collusion is impossible. The test holds outsider behaviour under reciprocal behaviour: in the ring round the trio does no work for the outsiders, so the outsiders point at each other; in the honest round the trio helps outsiders, so the outsiders point back at them. With outsider behaviour held fixed, a ring that only feeds itself would capture more, not less. If the whole round colludes, the maths cannot save it. The maths makes closed loop giving costly. It does not make it impossible. Coordinape said the same about its own model: it could be gamed and had blind spots, and worked best where a circle had already built trust. FairShare says the same, in plain sight.

Popularity bias remains too. Well liked members attract points. The cap and the square root soften it. They do not remove it. Sybil resistance comes from DAO member curation, not from the maths: the contract cannot tell a real member from a second account, and the DAO decides who is a member.
